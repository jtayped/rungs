## the idea

every recipe blog makes you read about someone's life before it tells you how much flour. this ladder keeps that form and asks the life story to be a confession.

## the turn

rules 1 to 5 build the post anyone would write. a fond memory, a dish made exactly once a year on one particular date, a recipe that works. the once-a-year rule looks like a seasonal detail. it is the anniversary.

rule 6 asks for a crime the writer got away with. the memory you wrote fondly is still there, and so is the date. rule 7 then asks the numbered method to double as the steps of the crime, while rule 2 still insists the recipe works. that is the collision. "leave to set, undisturbed, for as long as it takes" has to be true of the jelly and of something else.

the last rule takes the remorse out. a confession with regret in it is a different genre. without it you have a cheerful blogger recommending their crime to the readers and signing off.

## the words

the first rule asks whether this is the post itself, in the blogger's voice. a third-person description of such a post scored 0.98 on "written in the form of a blog post" and 0.31 on this wording.

the banned list comes after the confession rung, not before, so it bites the draft you have. "secret", "confess", "crime", "guilt" and "caught" are the words that label what the story is, and the labels have to go. the story has to be a confession without ever calling itself one.

the judge asks whether the story reveals a crime the writer got away with, not whether it confesses wrongdoing. a story about passing off a tin's recipe as nana's scored 0.77 on wrongdoing and 0.09 on crime. the hint says nobody was killed. a killing is the easy confession, and this ladder wants something smaller that the writer could still be charged for.

one practical thing. the game treats a draft as spam if any word appears more than three times, and recipes repeat ingredient names and step numbers. a 220-word post sits right on that line, which is why the hint about repetition is there.
