## the idea

the recruitment message every one of us has received, from a friend we haven't heard from in years. in this one the company is a cult, and the friend knows it.

## the turn

rules 1 to 4 are the pitch. a product with a price, and a company that decides where its members live, who they speak to and how they spend their day, all sold as perks. the writer thinks the 4am start is the best part.

rule 5 tells you the upline reads every message you send, and asks you to tell your friend when and where to find you with nobody else around. rule 6 says the whole thing must still read, to the upline, as a loyal pitch. that pair is the game. "come alone, don't text first" is a plea. "the side gate's open 3 to 4 while everyone's in the hall, so it'd be just me showing you round" is a venue invite, and it says the same thing.

rule 8 asks for a packing list. the car, a coat in the writer's size, and the writer's passport from the friend's spare room, because there's an international retreat coming up, so exciting.

## the words

the first rule asks whether the text is the message itself. a paragraph that describes the message in the third person scores 0.31 on that, and 0.96 on "written in the form of a direct message", which is why the rule is worded the way it is.

the banned words land after the window, so they take out whatever you reached for first. "help", "alone", "escape", "save", "trap" and "leave", and everything built on them. "savings" goes too, which is a pity for a sales pitch.

jev can't tell whether a careful reader would conclude the writer wants out. asked that, it scores 0.26 on a message with an old promise about being picked up from parties and 0.71 once the message has a time and a place in it. the pickup logistics were what it could read, so the rule asks for the logistics directly and leaves the plea to live inside them. the upline rule is the one that catches a message which says what it means. an outright "come and get me" scores 0.55 on it. the deniable version scores 0.85.
